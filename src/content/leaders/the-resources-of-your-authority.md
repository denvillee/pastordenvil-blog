Isaiah describes a coming king, and then he describes the people who lead under
him.

> Look, a righteous king is coming! And honest princes will rule under him. Each
> one will be like a shelter from the wind and a refuge from the storm, like
> streams of water in the desert and the shadow of a great rock in a parched
> land.

Shelter. Refuge. Water. Shade. Every one of those is a thing you are for
somebody else. None of them is a thing you are for yourself. A rock does not
need its own shadow.

## Where leaders get their cues

James and John asked for the seats on the right and the left. The other ten were
furious, which tells you they wanted the same thing. Jesus does not scold the
ambition. He relocates it.

> You know that those who are regarded as rulers of the Gentiles lord it over
> them, and their high officials exercise authority over them. Not so with you.

The Romans were in power. They were known for their leadership and their
military, modeled after the Babylonians and the Egyptians, and it was a
hierarchical structure. It wasn't the organization that was problematic, it was
the way they ruled.

That is worth sitting with, because it takes away the easy version of this
teaching. Jesus is not against structure. He is against a particular way of
holding it.

And here is the part that should make any of us nervous: being lorded over sets
an example for what kind of leader you are going to be. The disciples had been
under Rome their whole lives. When they finally imagined authority, they
imagined Rome, because that was the only authority they had ever seen. Not so
with you.

## You cannot be shade while you are still earning your standing

This is where Isaiah and Galatians meet.

> I have been crucified with Christ and I no longer live, but Christ lives in me.

Paul is making clear that right relationship with God is not based on
performance. Right relationship with God is based on God making it right.

The law was not the enemy in that argument. The law was made to sustain your
life, not to give you life. It was like a babysitter; its job was to keep you
alive until Christ came. But when Christ came, the babysitter was still in the
house, and a lot of us are still taking instructions from it.

Leaders do this with their leadership. We treat the role as the thing that
establishes us. Then we need the people we are leading to supply something:
proof, agreement, gratitude, a good quarter. And a leader who needs something
from you cannot be shelter for you. He is standing in your light, not casting
shade over you, because he is still trying to get warm himself.

Your identity comes from him. Your authority comes from him. Your leadership
style must also come from him.

## The question to take into the room

Somewhere in these notes I wrote down the question I actually want to lead by,
and I have not improved on it since:

> How can I take the resources of my authority and bring them into someone
> else's world for their benefit?

That is what the shelter is made of. Not warmth, not tone, not being liked.
Resources. Access, budget, cover, reputation, the benefit of the doubt, the
meeting you can get that they cannot. Authority is a set of resources, and the
question is only ever which direction you are pointing them.

Jesus does this all the time. Takes the resources of his authority, brings it
into your world for your benefit.

You are not the righteous king in Isaiah 32. You are one of the princes ruling
under him, and the whole reason you have anything to spend is that somebody
already made you right without asking you to perform for it.

So spend it. What can I do to help you?
