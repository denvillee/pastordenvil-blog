A few summers ago I asked our prayer team a question I had been carrying for a long time. Who do you think you are, and what do you think you're doing? I asked it because identity precedes activity. Who we think we are shapes what we think we are doing, in a prayer room and everywhere else. If you believe you are a volunteer filling a slot, you will pray like one. If you believe you are a priest standing where heaven and earth meet, you will pray differently, even if the words are the same.

That question sends you back to the beginning, because Genesis 1 tells us what a human is before it tells us what went wrong with one. And I have become convinced that a great deal of our trouble, in the church and outside it, comes from answering the question too small.

## The answer that is too small

Ask most Christians what makes a human a human and you will get some version of Genesis 2:7. God formed the man from the dust of the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and the man became a living being. Body plus breath. A physical organism animated by God. When the body has breath, it is a human. When the breath leaves, it is not.

There is nothing false in that. It is a floor, and I am grateful for it. But it is not the picture, and the text itself will not let us stop there. The phrase translated "living being" is *nephesh chayyah*, and Genesis uses the very same phrase for the fish and the birds on the fifth day and for the land animals on the sixth (Genesis 1:20, 24), and again for every animal the man names (Genesis 2:19). Ecclesiastes says it out loud: humans and animals "all have the same breath" (Ecclesiastes 3:19). Breath is not what makes us distinct. It is what we share with the sparrow. Joel Green points out that *nephesh* is not a thing a person has but a way a person is, and that Genesis 2:7 is better read as "the human being became fully alive" than as the moment a soul got installed.[^1]

So if you build your whole definition of the human on body and breath, you have built it on the one thing Scripture says we hold in common with the livestock. That is not a high view of humanity. It is the lowest view the Bible will permit, mistaken for the whole.

## What Genesis 1 actually says

Genesis 1 does not define us by what we are made of. It defines us by what we are made *as*, and what we are made *for*.

"Then God said, 'Let us make humankind in our image, according to our likeness, so that they may rule…'" (Genesis 1:26). Carmen Imes makes a case I find persuasive that the Hebrew preposition here is best read "as" rather than "in." God does not make humans and then put his image inside them, like a feature that could be removed. He makes humans *as* his image. The image is not a capacity we possess, not intelligence or morality or a soul, and so it is not something the fall could take from us or something a disability could diminish. It is who we are.[^2]

The ancient world would have understood this immediately, because that world was full of images. An idol was an image, the place where a god's presence was housed on earth, and a king was called the image of his god, the living statue of the god's rule in the land. Genesis takes that vocabulary and does something no one in the ancient Near East would have dared. It says every human being, male and female, is the image. Not the king. Not the priest. The farmer, the servant, the child. God planted his images in a garden that turns out to be a temple, the place where heaven and earth overlap, and humans were made to live in that overlap and to be it, dirt creatures animated by divine breath.[^3]

That is identity. But Genesis 1:26 does not stop there. The sentence has a purpose clause in it. God makes humans as his image *so that* they may rule, and then he blesses them and tells them to be fruitful, to fill, to subdue, to have dominion. The image is not only something we are. It is something we are called to do. When Genesis 2 puts the man in the garden "to work it and keep it," the two Hebrew verbs, *abad* and *shamar*, are the same two verbs used later for what priests do in the tabernacle (Numbers 3:7-8). Work in the garden is worship. Tending creation is priestly service. The image of God is something we are before it is something we do, but it is never only something we are. It is identity and activity, and the Bible refuses to separate them.[^4]

Green puts it this way: Genesis "does not define humanity in essentialist terms but in relational, as Yahweh's partner."[^5] The definition of a human has stopped being a question about ingredients and become a question about relationship and calling.

## Not good to be alone

There is one more thing Genesis will not let us leave out. In a chapter where God says "good" over and over, the first "not good" is a human being by himself. "It is not good that the man should be alone" (Genesis 2:18). The man had a body. He had breath. He had a vocation and a garden and the presence of God. By the body-and-breath definition he was fully human already, and God looked at him and said, not yet.

Even the grammar of Genesis 1:26 is plural. "Let *us* make humankind in *our* image." Whatever else that plural means, it means the God whose image we are is not solitary, and so the image is not solitary either. Michelle Gonzalez says it simply: "The human being is not self-contained but rather is constituted by relationships."[^6] Desmond Tutu said the same thing from another continent: a person is a person through other persons.

When I wrote about this in seminary I landed on three phrases for what Genesis adds to body and breath: purposeful responsibility, intimate relationship with God, and the centrality of community. To be human is to be made as God's image, sent into his world with a job, kept in his presence, and set among other people. Take any one of them away and the text says something is not good.

## The body is not the problem

There is a way of saying "more than body and breath" that ends up meaning "less body." The church has done that for centuries. We inherited from Plato and Descartes and a hundred bad sermons the idea that the real me is a soul and the body is the container, and that spirituality is the art of getting out of it. That is not Genesis, and it is not the gospel. God made dirt and called it good. God made a body and breathed into it and called it very good. And when God came to rescue us, he did not send a message. He took a body.

Hillary McBride, a therapist, writes that "the body is not something you have but something you are," and she notices what should have been obvious to us, that "bodies alive are bodies breathed." The first thing a baby does on this side of the world is inhale, and the last thing a body does is exhale.[^7] Cole Arthur Riley, thinking about Genesis, writes that God says, "Not out of my own womb but out of this here dust will I make you," and she will have nothing to do with "any call to spirituality that divorces my mind from my body, voice, or people."[^8]

So the high view I am after is not a view that floats above the body. It is a view that starts with the body, refuses to end there, and refuses to leave it behind. Body and breath are where the image lives. They are not what the image is.

## Why the size of the definition matters

A small definition of the human produces a small ethic. If a human is only body and breath, then the only moral question we know how to ask is when body and breath begin, and the only thing we know how to defend is the beginning. We become very good at guarding the doorway and very quiet about the house. But if a human is God's image, made for vocation, presence, and community, then every place where a person is denied work, or presence, or belonging is a place where the image is being defaced, and the church has something to say there too. Riley's line is the one I keep: "To be human in an aching world is to know our dignity and become people who safeguard the dignity of everything around us."[^9]

And there is a kingdom shape to this. The New Testament calls Jesus "the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15) and says that the point of our salvation is to be "conformed to the image of his Son" (Romans 8:29). The writer of Hebrews reads Psalm 8, the psalm that crowns every human with glory and honor, and says we do not yet see everything under humanity's feet, "but we see Jesus" (Hebrews 2:8-9). Being human is not only where we came from. It is where we are going. Genesis 1 is a promise as much as an origin story, and the one fully human life we have ever seen is the life of Christ. We are not humans trying to become spiritual. We are images being brought, in Christ, to the full stature of what a human was always meant to be.

Who do you think you are? You are the image of God, in a body, with a job, kept in his presence, set among his people, on your way to looking like Jesus. That is not a definition you could read off a heartbeat monitor. It is the one the Bible gives. And what you think you are doing, in a prayer room, at a kitchen table, will follow from it.
